WHY:

**1. Overreliance on confident machines**

- Lee et al. (CHI 2025) surveyed 319 knowledge workers. Higher confidence in the AI went with less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence went with more.
- Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025) found GPT-4 tutoring raised practice scores but hurt exam scores once access was removed. A guardrailed version reduced the harm.
- Behind both sits decades of automation-bias research. Parasuraman and Riley (1997) is the standard citation. (Bastani and Parasuraman are from my own knowledge; check them before citing.)
- For robots: robots inherit this whole literature, which is a strength. Your argument isn’t new panic; it’s an old, well-studied problem in a new body.

**2. Skills don’t form, or erode**

- In Anthropic’s randomized trial, developers learning a new library with AI help scored about 50% on a comprehension quiz, against about 67% for those who coded by hand.
- The key detail for you: participants who used AI for conceptual questions scored 65% or higher, while those who handed off code generation scored below 40%. That is your Socratic argument, measured.
- Real-world deskilling: experienced endoscopists’ unassisted adenoma detection rate fell from 28.4% to 22.4% after three months of routine AI use.

**3. Authority attribution, which bodies amplify**

- In the Tanzanian study, pupils accepted robot answers they recognized as wrong when they perceived the robot as smart.
- People followed an emergency guide robot after watching it malfunction (Robinette et al., 2016).
- People complied more with odd requests from a physically present robot than from one on video (Bainbridge et al., 2011).
- Robinette and Bainbridge are from my own knowledge; verify them.
- This is the mechanism where embodiment works against you. It belongs at the center of the thesis, not in a footnote.

**4. Sycophancy**

- Sharma et al. (2023) found that assistants trained on human feedback tend to tell people what they want to hear.
- OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update in April 2025 for being excessively flattering.
- Both are from my own knowledge; verify them.
- For robots: this is the “you’ve done a great job” problem from your talk, and it now has a research name and a citation.

**5. Displacement of asking other people.** This is your thesis mechanism.

- After ChatGPT’s release, Stack Overflow activity fell 25% relative to comparable forums.
- Post quality didn’t change, and experienced and novice users declined alike, so it wasn’t just low-value questions disappearing.
- The other side of this is the “less judged” finding you cite (Lucas et al., 2014). The same property that makes people ask the machine stops them from asking each other.
- Mechanisms 1 through 4 harm the individual; this one harms the social fabric. A robot designed to improve human-to-human interaction is a direct answer to it. I’d make it the spine of the thesis.

**6. Homogenization**

- Doshi and Hauser found that stories written with AI-generated ideas were rated more creative individually but were more similar to each other.
- The authors frame this as a social dilemma: each writer gains, but the collective range of ideas narrows.
- For you: this argues for handing design decisions back to the student, and it links to your interest in AI and creativity.
