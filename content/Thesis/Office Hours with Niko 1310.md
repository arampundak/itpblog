**Where I am.** I started the semester slowly, and something shifted about two weeks ago. Part of it was realizing that my thesis grows mostly through talking: explaining the ideas out loud and hearing myself explain them. Part of it was finding a concrete vehicle. The HRI 2027 Student Design Competition has the theme “Robots in Public Spaces,” and I’m entering it with Ari, a shop tutor robot, as my Project Development work. Ari isn’t my thesis. It’s the first step toward it.

**Why I care.** I don’t think AI is harmless, but **my argument is that the harm depends on the interaction design**, not the technology. We already sense what’s wrong with how we interact with AI today. **It’s overly agreeable, it doesn’t learn how to talk to a specific person, and it rarely admits what it doesn’t know**. ==As AI moves into physical objects, I don’t want robots to repeat those mistakes.== Ari’s small version of the answer is that it acknowledges people, invites them in, and hands off to a human when it isn’t the authority.

**Material.** I want to move Ari away from the white-plastic, medical look and toward wood. Niko’s advice was practical. Thin milled solid-wood shells will warp and crack. Plywood, veneer and compression-molded shells are more stable. If I want solid wood, I should design thicker, assembled forms, closer to teenage engineering’s Choir dolls. Wood combined with visible plastic, or even cast bronze, could look unlike anything else out there. And ==I should run small material experiments now==, in parallel with research, instead of waiting.

**Story over spec.** I worried about ending my thesis with a promotional video. Niko’s view was that the difference between a promo and a film is just how good it is. The power comes from the human relationship with the robot, not its features. The same applies to the ==thesis presentation: think of it as pitching a script to producers, with storyboards.== Don’t show a finished film or explain the transmission. Technical detail belongs on the website and in the paper.

**Ideas he gave me.**

- **The Waymo gap.** When you stand in front of a driverless car, how does it tell you that you can cross? That empty space between person and machine is a familiar entry point into my whole topic.
- **Pare it back.** How minimal can the robot get before it stops doing what it’s meant to do? ==Sometimes a flag that drops is more human than a robot that talks without really conversing.==
- **Who it belongs to.** ==Ownership, scale, mobility and fragmentation all change how we behave around a robot. One that belongs to the shop is different from one that belongs to me.== One robot is different from many small ones sharing a brain.
- **Seeing and listening.** ==Any robot that watches and listens raises the question of where the data goes, and today there’s no intuitive way to show that.==
- **Bring people in early.** Niko’s own turning point was a single workshop. ==Short sessions with a few people, some clay and a question produce objects,== interviews and documentation all at once.
- **Plan documentation early.** Block a week, rent a space, bring lights and people, write a script.

### What matters most for my thesis

1. **The question of minimal character.** Paring back challenges my own direction in a useful way. My framework has to say when a robot shouldn’t talk, move or perform, not just how to add character.
2. **Ownership, scale and loyalty as design dimensions.** ==Who the robot serves shapes the interaction as much as its form does.== Ari serves the shop, and that needs addressing directly.
3. **Surveillance in human-robot-human interaction.** ===If robots shape how people treat each other, the line between facilitation, paternalism and surveillance is the central risk.== It isn’t a side note.
4. **Comparing robots is a design inquiry, not an experiment.** ==Running the same personality on 3, 6 and 36 degrees of freedom changes too many things at once to prove anything about personality.== It’s still valuable for documenting what survives and what breaks.
5. **Making and researching in parallel.** There won’t be a clean moment when research ends and execution begins, especially with the timeline I have.
6. **Story as the deliverable.** The ==thesis is the argument and the framework, told through people. It isn’t a polished product.==

### What I want to ask in my thesis

Embodied AI is about to spread into everyday objects and public spaces, and I believe the harm or good it does will come from how its interactions are designed, not from the technology itself. My thesis asks how we should design the character of a robot so that it improves the interactions between people rather than replacing or quietly degrading them. ==That means a robot that knows its limits, hands off to people when it should, and makes people feel acknowledged rather than managed.== Part of that question is how much character a given context needs, from a signal as simple as a flag to a robot that speaks. Another part is how ownership, scale and visibility of what the robot sees and hears change that answer. I want to turn this into a framework for designing robot character in shared spaces, starting with Ari in the shop and extending to places like libraries and waiting rooms.
