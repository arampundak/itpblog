# What Robotics Founders Get Wrong Before They Ever Talk to a Designer

## Full text

I've talked with dozens of robotics founders. Different markets, different technologies, different stages. The same five issues come up, over and over.

You're waiting on design until after the raise. Investors want to see the vision before they fund it. The founders who move fastest treat design as a fundraising tool, not a reward for closing.

Your timeline is too clean. Hardware investors are pattern-matchers. A Gantt chart with no room for error doesn't signal confidence. It signals that you haven't fully reckoned with the complexity. Honest uncertainty reads as sophistication.

You haven't stress-tested your physical constraints. Heat dissipation, sealing, weight, wiring. These interact in ways that only become visible when you look at the system as a whole. Get interdisciplinary eyes on your hardware early, before the architecture is locked.

You're staying on the OTS bridge too long. Off-the-shelf components are a smart wedge. But dependency on a third-party platform caps your unit economics, your differentiation, and your demo schedule. The bridge has a weight limit. Know when it ends.

Your north star is not your product. "A humanoid for home or industrial use" is a vision. "A companion robot designed specifically for senior living" is a fundable thesis. The wedge gets you to the north star. Make it specific enough to defend.

One more, and it might be the most important:
Design is not the last step. It isn't the cosmetic layer you apply to a working prototype. The companies that treat design as strategic from day one, the ones that let it shape architecture decisions and stress-test use cases early, end up with better products and stronger raises.

Building a robot is one of the hardest things a founder can do. The physics don't negotiate. But the patterns that trip teams up are more predictable than the engineering problems themselves.

See them early enough, and you can build around them.
