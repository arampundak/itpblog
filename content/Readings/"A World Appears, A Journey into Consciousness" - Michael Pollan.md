Chapter 3, "Thought," from _A World Appears: A Journey into Consciousness_, explores how little we actually know about the contents of our own minds, since most theories of consciousness focus on perception and leave thought itself almost unexamined. The author draws on William James's account of the "stream of thought" as continuous, fringed, and never repeating, then submits himself to Russell Hurlburt's beeper-driven Descriptive Experience Sampling method, which reveals how hard it is to pin down whether a given moment of thought was verbal, visual, or something else — and forces him to confront the "observer effect," where trying to catch a thought changes it. He also examines Christoff Hadjiilieva's neuroscience of spontaneous thought and mind-wandering, including her finding that unconscious brain activity precedes a spontaneous thought by several seconds and her critique of science's cultural bias toward "productive" thinking. The chapter closes with a tour of stream-of-consciousness literature (James Joyce, Virginia Woolf, Tolstoy, and novelist Lucy Ellmann), arguing that novelists were mapping the terrain of thought long before scientists took it seriously. Throughout, the throughline is that every method for studying thought — scientific, introspective, or literary — inevitably distorts or oversimplifies the very thing it tries to capture.
