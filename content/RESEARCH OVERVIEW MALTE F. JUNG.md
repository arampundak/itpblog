by [[MALTE JUNG]]
a 2019 snapshot

>yet research has been focusing almost exclusively on understanding and enabling interactions between a single robot and a single human.

>design techniques for robots to deliberately shape a group’s interaction as well as its social and emotional processes... from focusing on interactions between people and a technology to questions of how technology affects the way people interact with each other 

1. build basic understanding and theory about human robot interaction and a robot’s impact on groups and teams.
2. techniques for designing robots that function effectively in teams and that can improve team dynamics and team effectiveness. 
3. expanding the methodological toolkit... by introducing experiment tasks that facilitate the systematic study of a robot’s impact on groups and teams.  

how robots influence group dynamics and what the consequences of such influence are (Jung and Hinds, 2018)

> We found that robots can ==impact basic group processes such as conflict== (Jung, Martelaro, & Hinds, 2015; Stoll, Jung, & Fussell, 2018), ==decision making== (Hou, Lee, & Jung, In Prep), or group participation (Tennent, Shen, & Jung, 2018). These studies formed the empirical basis for our efforts to synthesize what we have learned into general theoretical frameworks on robots in group settings (e.g. Shen, Tennent, & Jung, 2017). 

>The second thread of my work focuses on the question of how we design robots that operate effectively in teams. I have been particularly interested in designing robots and systems that act as social catalysts for teams. Our goal is to design robots that use subtle social nudges to help teams perform at their best. 

**Three threads:**

1. **Theory: robots shape human-human dynamics.**
    - His dissertation claimed that exchanges of emotional expression accounted for up to 91% of a team’s performance.
    - Lab studies found robots affect conflict, decision-making, and participation.
    - The key finding: a plain industrial arm created interpersonal tension between teammates just through _how_ it handed out blocks. So even robots with no social design shape how people treat each other, not just how they treat the robot.
    - With Scassellati’s lab at Yale, a robot that expressed vulnerability (“sorry, I made a mistake”) made group members more supportive of each other.
    - Field work on the da Vinci surgical robot found it reorganized OR teamwork. It isolated the surgeon physically and changed how requests were made and answered.
2. **Design: robots as “social catalysts.”**
    - Early work used robots as human-like moderators that backchanneled, intervened in conflict, or expressed vulnerability.
    - Jung names three problems with that approach:
        - Human-likeness sets expectations the robot can’t meet, which erodes trust.
        - Building a capable moderator is technically expensive.
        - An active robot pulls attention away from the group and the task.
    - So he pivoted to simple, non-anthropomorphic objects acting from the periphery. **Micbot** is the example: a microphone that turns and tilts toward quieter people. It significantly balanced participation and improved team performance compared to a motionless mic.
3. **Methods: group tasks for HRI studies.**
    - Tower construction, where a robot arm distributes blocks between people.
    - An online Tetris version with a virtual robot allocating pieces.
    - A simulated telepresence task.
    - These exist because standard social-psych group tasks don’t map onto robots.
