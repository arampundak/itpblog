By [[Matt Shumar]]  • Feb 9, 2026

### Core Message

The author argues that we are at the beginning of an AI transformation that is moving far faster — and will be far more disruptive — than most people realize. They compare today’s AI moment to February 2020 before COVID lockdowns: things seemed normal, warnings sounded exaggerated, and then everything changed very quickly.

Their claim is that we’re in the “this seems overblown” phase of something much bigger.

---

### What Changed (From the Author’s Perspective)

The author works in AI and says the shift has already happened in tech:

- AI moved from being a helpful tool to doing full jobs independently.
    
- New 2025–2026 models (e.g., advanced coding models) can:
    
    - Build complete applications from plain English descriptions
        
    - Test, refine, and improve their own output
        
    - Make decisions that resemble “judgment” or “taste”
        
- AI is now being used to help build the next generation of AI — accelerating progress.
    

They describe this as the start of a feedback loop where AI improves itself, speeding up development.

---

### The Big Claim

The author believes:

- Within 1–5 years (possibly sooner), AI could replace a large percentage of entry-level white-collar jobs.
    
- This includes law, finance, writing, analysis, software engineering, consulting, and more.
    
- Anything that primarily happens on a computer is exposed.
    
- This wave is different from past automation because AI improves across _all cognitive domains simultaneously_.
    

They cite rapid improvement in AI capability — moving from basic arithmetic errors (2022) to professional-level outputs (2024–2026) — and argue the pace is accelerating, not slowing.

---

### Why Many People Don’t See It

The author says the public underestimates AI because:

- They tried early versions (2023–2024) that were weaker.
    
- Many people only use free versions, which lag behind cutting-edge models.
    
- Most people treat AI like a search engine instead of integrating it into real work.
    

Meanwhile, leaders in industries who seriously test advanced models are reportedly “blown away” and preparing accordingly.

---

### What They Recommend

Rather than panic, the author advises:

1. **Start using AI seriously** — daily, deeply, and for real tasks.
    
2. **Adapt early** — early adopters gain career advantage.
    
3. **Build financial resilience** — prepare for possible disruption.
    
4. **Lean into harder-to-replace areas** — relationships, accountability, regulated roles.
    
5. **Teach adaptability** — especially to children.
    
6. **Spend one hour a day experimenting with AI tools.**
    

They frame this moment as both a threat and a massive opportunity:

- Anyone can now build apps, write books, learn new skills cheaply.
    
- Barriers to creating things are collapsing.
    

---

### Bigger Picture (Beyond Jobs)

The author expands the scope:

- AI could transform medicine and science dramatically.
    
- It could also pose national security risks.
    
- Some AI labs warn of “intelligence explosion” dynamics.
    
- The upside and downside are both enormous.
    

---

### Overall Tone

The piece is urgent, almost alarmist — but framed as a warning from someone inside the field who feels the public gap between perception and reality is dangerous.

It’s not predicting a distant future.

It’s arguing: _“This has already started. You just haven’t felt it yet.”_
