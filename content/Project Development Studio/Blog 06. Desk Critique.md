During Desk Critique with Pedro we mainly talked about the design of the robot. I was very intrigued to choose a robust squared shape and Pedro pushed me to explore what people actually find compelling. After doing some AB testing I understood that the normal user find the original rounded shape to bo more compelling... 
That put me in a dilemma - regarding the Nature Like ↔ Machine Like axis - I prefer the robot design to be more machine like, as it lives in the shop area and has a shop staff Socratic persona. Knowing what most users prefer makes me afraid to push towards "design for designers".
I also took some time to understand [[Reachy Mini Software]] by doing [[Reachy Mini Experiment 2]]
### Design for designers

“You are not the user.” from Jakob Nielsen and Nielsen Norman Group. 
Their framing is that designers, developers and engineers project their own behavior and reactions onto users. The phrase is rooted in a social psychology bias where people naturally assume that others share their own mental models, habits, and preferences.
### Points from conversation with Pedro

**Expected form**  
People come to a robot with a mental image of what it should look like, and a design that departs from that image has to earn the departure.

**Smooth = approachable**  
Pedro’s core point: smooth, rounded surfaces read as friendly and safe, which matters for a robot that strangers are supposed to walk up to.

**Rigid is already close to something**  
Rigid, boxy forms tend to read as close to an existing object, like an appliance or a machine, rather than as a character.

**Machine-like ↔ nature-like**  
Form sits on a spectrum. My boxy direction pushes Ari toward the machine end, while rounded forms pull it toward something more organic and alive.

**My pitch was about people’s perception of form**  
My original pitch centered on how people perceive a robot’s form, so the shape decision has to be grounded in that, not only in my own taste.

**Implementation does not meet the box**  
The current build doesn’t yet deliver on the boxy idea: what I’ve made so far doesn’t express the machine-like intent clearly.

[[Paula Gaetano Adi]]
A reference Pedro suggested. She’s an Argentine artist working in robotics, sculpture and performance. Her robotic works express minimal signs of a living body, such as sweating and breathing, which connects to the next point.

**Animation changes our perception**  
How a robot moves can change how its form is read. A rigid shape can feel alive through motion, so form and behavior shouldn’t be judged separately.

**Knowledge related to the shop**  
Ari’s value comes from knowing the ITP shop: its machines, procedures and safety rules.

**General intelligence vs. specific knowledge**  
A specialist robot that knows one place deeply is a different proposition from a general assistant, and the form could signal that specificity.

==**Having a case**  
Whatever form I choose, I need a clear case for it: an argument I can defend, not just a preference.==

**Why are people going in a soft direction in robot design?**  
Pedro’s question to me: there’s a reason the industry has moved toward soft, rounded robots, and I should understand it before going against it.

**Desirable robot**  
The robot should be something people want to be near and interact with, not just something that functions.

**Round = more approachable**  
Pedro came back to the same point: rounded forms make people more willing to approach.

#### Next steps:

- **Which place would have it:** decide where Ari actually lives (the shop desk, my desk, or the entrance), because the setting should shape the form.
- **Perception: what looks more heavy:** test how people read the visual weight of different forms. A box can look heavy and static, and a rounded form lighter.
- **Justify:** whichever direction I take, back it with evidence and reasoning.
