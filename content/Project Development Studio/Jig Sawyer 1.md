**What if the shop could talk back?** Jig Sawyer, Jr. is a small wooden robot built on the Reachy Mini that lives in the ITP shop, a shared workshop in the middle of a busy floor. People from every background come there to make things with tools that can be loud, intimidating and dangerous. Today, when staff are busy, newcomers get a QR code that links to written instructions. Jig Sawyer, Jr. extends the shop staff instead: a portable, lantern-like companion that a maker carries to the laser cutter or the 3D printer. Once there, he acts as a Socratic teacher. He doesn't hand over answers or settings. He asks what you're trying to make and what you've tried, explains why things work, and has you do each step yourself, so the judgment stays with you. He draws only on the shop's own documentation and staff knowledge, is firm on safety, and hands anything risky to a human. Because the shop is shared, he speaks only when someone asks him to, and he asks permission before using his camera to remember anyone. Passers-by and people working nearby are never pulled into a conversation they didn't choose. The project responds to a growing reliance on AI that does the work for us and wears away skill, creativity and the ability to face a challenge. Its premise is that AI should take the tedious parts and leave people the interesting decisions. Success means a newcomer can run a machine alone on their second visit

Some feedback:
- A robot that works out why a print failed, checks users' files and fixes common issues is handing out fish. Most of what you describe is monitoring and diagnosis. The robot should notice a failed print and _ask the user_ what they think went wrong, not tell them.
- Your actual project is a persona on one embodied robot, and the post never ties back to it.
- Treat the body as the interface. Voice teaches. Motion and light carry state and safety, and they're designed to be read by everyone on the floor, not just the maker.
- Socratic method
