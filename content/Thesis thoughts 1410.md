Generative AI arrived quickly, and we are already seeing how its interaction patterns shape us. Embodied AI is the next step, and agentic AI accelerates it. When a machine takes up physical space, its way of interacting stops being a matter of interface and becomes a matter of social behavior. A child who learns to command a voice assistant, or a parent with a stroller who cannot tell whether an autonomous car sees them, illustrates the same gap: we are building machines that share our spaces without giving them the social signals that make shared space work. My thesis asks how the character and behavior of embodied AI, including how it shows attention, intent, and what it perceives, can be designed so that robots adapt to human social norms rather than humans adapting to robots, and whether such robots can strengthen the interactions between the people around them. I focus on social harm. I argue that much of it is a product of interaction design rather than an inevitable property of the technology, and I investigate it through research by making, building and testing robots in shared, semi-public spaces.
