Five years ago, my bachelor's graduation project started from one observation: children who grow up with voice assistants learn to speak in a commanding manner. It led me to redesign Alexa with different avatars and personalities, and the story has stayed with me since. Technology doesn't only serve us. It trains us.

Generative AI is the next chapter of that story. It will make anything we ask for, instantly, and we are learning to accept whatever it hands us. At HRI 2026, researchers working with LLM-powered robot tutors found that [pupils accepted the robot's answers even when they recognized them as wrong](https://imec-publications.be/entities/publication/6f9635a9-7a3a-4fa6-b032-8d4a58e81cad/full), as long as the robot seemed smart.

I believe AI should take the repetitive work and leave the decisions to us. Robots matter here because they share our physical space: a body can show where to look, who it is talking to, and when a choice belongs to you. My question is how to design robots, and the way we interact with them, so that they hand decisions back to people instead of taking them away.

## What HRI is

[The ACM/IEEE International Conference on Human-Robot Interaction](https://humanrobotinteraction.org/2027/) is the main annual venue for research on how people and robots work, live and communicate together; the 2027 edition runs March 8–12 in Santa Clara, California.

## The ask

There are several different tracks to participate in the conference [[HRI Conference ACM IEEE International]]. ==The one I found suiting me the most is the **SDC - Student Design Competition**.==
The competition asks student teams to ==design a robot or interactive system for a real public space==, and to show not only what they designed but why. 
This year's theme is ==**Robots in Public Spaces**: sidewalks, parks, museums, libraries, transit stations, hospital lobbies and other shared institutional spaces.== In those places a robot meets strangers rather than trained users, bystanders as well as intended users, crowds, queues, and people who never asked to interact with a robot at all.

**A project counts as on theme when:**
- it is ==set in a specific, named public or quasi-public place==, not "anywhere";
- it ==accounts for more than one party==: users, bystanders, passers-by, staff;
- some key design choices exist _because_ the place is public;
- it ==deals with at least one real complication==, such as crowding, noise, uninvited interaction, accessibility or consent.

==**What teams submit:** a four-page, design-focused paper and an optional three-minute video.== A physical robot, software, an interaction concept or a service design are all accepted. Prototypes can be low-fidelity or remotely operated, as long as that is disclosed. Accepted teams demo live at the conference.

**How it is judged:** on design quality, the reasoning behind decisions, and how seriously the project engages with public-space realities, not on technical complexity. Expensive hardware gives no advantage.

**Dates:** **submission November 20, 2026**; decisions December 18, 2026 (my birthday!).

## Draft Abstract What if the shop could talk back?

Makerspaces are spreading through schools, libraries and cities because they hand people the rod, not the fish. People with very different backgrounds come to learn by doing, with their hands. The catch is that they put powerful machines in front of people who have rarely used them, and beginners don't know what they don't know. I work in a university shop, and it isn't my first. Newcomers are shown a laser cutter once at best, and when staff are busy they wait, guess, or give up. Many would rather ask a machine the question they are embarrassed to ask a person.

Ari is a small tutor robot for the ITP shop at NYU, built on Reachy Mini, an open-source desktop robot. It splits its help in two. It is direct about procedure and safety, and Socratic about design decisions: choices such as which material to use, or what to change after a test cut, go back to the student.

Because the shop is shared, Ari is designed for everyone in the room, not only the person using it. Its camera stays physically closed until the user opens it, and then looks only at the machine bed. It acknowledges people who are waiting and invites them to listen in. And it hands off to shop staff for the final check, so it adds to the people in the shop instead of replacing them.

_Work in progress. Ari is a working name, and feedback is welcome._

Key Phrases:
Learning by doing - learning with your hands, US manufacturing declined, don't know what they don't know, People ask an agent again and again because it won't judge them. Lucas et al. (2014), AI should take the repetitive tasks and leave the decisions to people, "Shrinking society": fewer people available to teach, A physical tutor teaches better than a screen

**Submission:**
- A paper of up to 4 pages including references, in ACM format. It is not anonymous.
- It's published as a non-archival companion paper.
- An optional video of up to 3 minutes is strongly encouraged.
- The paper must cover: setting, stakeholders, the interaction, goals and constraints, process and rationale (including alternatives you considered), expected user experience and failure modes, a demo plan, and at least one image.
- Unfinished work is allowed. You have to show what's done, what remains, and that it's realistic, and it must be complete by camera-ready.

**Judging:** 
Scenario clarity, handling of multiple parties and bystanders, depth of rationale, ethics and accessibility, how well the demo communicates, and whether the remaining work is feasible.
