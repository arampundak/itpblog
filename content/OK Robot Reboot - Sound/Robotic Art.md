My all-time favorite human-machine interaction guru is [[Guy Hoffman]]. His robot, [[Shimi Robotic Speaker Dock]] (2013), is one of those rare perfect concepts (I immediately fall in love). I consider it not just a product, but a design thought - an art piece - an idea - almost a virtue.

Shimi is a robotic smartphone speaker dock and music listening companion. It’s a cute tabletop device with a head composed of two speakers and body, two distinguished limbs in front - one functioning as an arm, the other as a leg - each with its own purpose (great use of asymmetry). The “arm” is a phone holder. The user places their Android device in Shimi’s hand, where it docks and charges. After selecting a song, the robot turns the phone toward itself and tilts its head as if to look at it - a small gesture that reads immediately as curiosity.

Then it lifts its head back toward the user and begins to play the music. While the song fills the room, Shimi taps its foot to the rhythm. The movement is simple, but it creates something powerful: a shared listening experience.

This is intentional expressiveness. Nothing is excessive. No face, no screen-based emotions, no synthetic voice. Just timing, posture, anticipation, and motion.

An art piece that plays on the edge of what we call robotic is [[David Bowen]]’s _Mars Wind Collaboration with NASA JPL (2024)_.

> The piece consists of 126 x/y tilting mechanical devices connected to tall dried grass stalks, installed like a field in the gallery. The mechanisms tilt, move, and sway based on data collected from the wind sensor aboard the Perseverance Mars Rover.

It continues a body of work that began with [[tele-present wind]]

The main character in this piece is not the machine. It is the wind. A sensor deployed on the robot — the Perseverance rover — sends readings from Mars. Those readings are mapped onto 126 individual units, each composed of two motors and a tall dried stalk. Together they form an array that moves with a flock-like rhythm. Each “pixel” has a three-dimensional movement in space, larger than the motors themselves suggest. The weight of the stalk lifts the center of mass upward; the drag introduces delay and anticipation. The mechanics are simple, but the physics create nuance.

It becomes a choreographed landscape — dense enough to feel like a field, sparse enough to feel displaced. The wind is not here, yet it is embodied here.

Bowen writes:

> “Thus, the individual components of the installation here on earth will move in unison as they mimic the direction and intensity of the wind from another planet.”

What is robotic here is not a humanoid form, but translation. A sensing machine on Mars becomes a physical gesture on Earth. The system relays, in Bowen’s words,

> “a physical representation of the dynamic and fluid environmental conditions.”

Robots have always been rooted in our past imagination of the future. They make us ask how we will feel about distant worlds, about technology, about mediation. In this piece, the robot is no longer a body — it is a bridge. Not a character with a face, but a system that makes the invisible move in front of us.
