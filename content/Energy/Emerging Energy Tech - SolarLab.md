**SolarLab

today I’m presenting SolarLab, a Danish company developing building-integrated photovoltaics, or BIPV. [Building-integrated photovoltaics](https://www.google.com/search?q=Building-integrated+photovoltaics&rlz=1C5OZZY_enUS1147US1148&oq=BIPV&gs_lcrp=EgZjaHJvbWUqBwgAEAAYgAQyBwgAEAAYgAQyBwgBEAAYgAQyBwgCEAAYgAQyCQgDEAAYChiABDIHCAQQABiABDIGCAUQRRhBMgYIBhBFGEEyBggHEEUYQdIBBzE5NmowajeoAgCwAgA&sourceid=chrome&ie=UTF-8&ved=2ahUKEwjR0dPQzOmTAxUGjIkEHVKVJIkQgK4QegYIAQgAEAY) (BIPV) are solar power systems seamlessly incorporated into a building's envelope

Instead of adding conventional solar panels onto a building, SolarLab turns the facade itself into an energy-generating system. Their products can function as rain-screens, curtain walls, louvers, and roofs, so they combine architecture, envelope performance, and electricity production in one system.

SolarLab BIPV facade

- optimized for multiple functions at once
    
- produces electricity and works as facade/cladding/rain-screen/shading
    
- can be used where roof area is limited
    
- gives architects more control over appearance
    
- usually sacrifices some efficiency for integration, color, orientation, and building function 
    

SolarLab panels do not make one fixed amount of electricity everywhere.

How much power they produce depends on where the building is, how the panels are placed, and how well the whole system is designed.

They use both roofs and facades as Denmark’s sun usually goes very low.

formula is basically:

Energy produced = sunlight × active solar area × panel power × real-world efficiency

output varies

- depends on sun, orientation, shading, active area, and system losses
    
- about 145 W peak per m²
    
- loses about 0.7% performance per year
    
- designed for about 25 years of electricity production
    

  

What makes SolarLab an emerging energy technology is that it integrates power generation into the architecture itself. It can free roof space, work in retrofit or new construction, and offer strong aesthetic flexibility. At the same time, it brings engineering tradeoffs: facade solar is more site-dependent than rooftop solar, and the manufacturing impacts are concentrated mainly in the production of the photovoltaic laminate and aluminum components. Overall, SolarLab shows how buildings can become part of the energy system, not just consumers of energy.**
