### **ROBOTDOG2 — Revised Narration**

**What if the machines around us were less like tools — and more like companions?**

**“We shape our tools, and thereafter our tools shape us.” — John M. Culkin**

As artificial intelligence moves from screens into physical space,  
we are entering a new phase of human–machine interaction.

This project asks a simple question:  
**what does it take for a machine to feel socially present?**

We set out to explore what it feels like to be seen by a robot,  
and how simple behavior can give a machine a sense of agency in human interaction.

We developed the project through a series of prototypes.  
We started with a simple three-degree-of-freedom robot,  
then added sensing and basic decision-making,  
and finally integrated more motors, a camera, and custom human-recognition software.

Along the way, we learned that improving behavior mattered more than adding intelligence.

Our research focused on empathy and companionship.  
We studied how dogs communicate through posture and movement,  
and moments where people help delivery robots stuck in public spaces,  
alongside academic research in human–robot interaction.

These examples showed us that people can empathize with machines — even very simple ones.

We treated software not as intelligence, but as a way to sense and respond.  
Using Python and MediaPipe, we explored attention and face tracking,  
and used Bottango to translate behavior into smooth, expressive motion.

For hardware, we studied expressive robotic systems, including work from Disney Research.  
Using abstract, non-humanoid forms, we designed a four-degree-of-freedom head capable of movements we intuitively read as social and friendly.

This project is not a product.  
It is an experiment.

We believe smart objects can cultivate good friction —  
and that as machines take on physical form,  
we must be intentional about how they move, react, and relate to us.
